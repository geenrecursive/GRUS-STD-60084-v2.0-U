How to Read This Standard¶

Parts I through III state the purpose, the statutory frame, and the reason self-reporting cannot satisfy an audit of resource consumption. Part IV supplies the engineering basis for the classification framework — the two axes against which any facility is read — stated entirely from published, vendor-neutral figures so that it can be applied by any reviewer without access to proprietary data.

Part V is the proposed rule. Each subsection appears in a bordered block as proposed rule text, followed by its basis in ordinary type. The bordered blocks, taken together and in order, are the complete proposed text of the section. Nothing outside a bordered block is proposed for adoption.

Part VI explains why each subsection is part of the measurement rather than an addition to it. Part VII is the request. The Technical Annexes are incorporated by reference at subsection (m) and are not appendices of convenience. Annex I in particular states every formula used anywhere in this document, together with the worked arithmetic, so that any reviewing engineer can reproduce or refute each number without contacting the author.

How the jurisdictional entries work. Everywhere this standard would otherwise cite the law of one jurisdiction, it states a numbered entry instead — J-1adopting authority through J-42counsel review — followed by a ruled space and, in parentheses, a description of what the space receives. The Schedule immediately below lists every entry once, with its default, so the adopting authority may complete the Schedule in a single pass and need not search the document for the spaces. Completing the Schedule completes the standard. A space left blank takes the default stated for it in the Schedule.

The entries are closed in number and closed in kind. No entry alters a classification on either axis, a measurement boundary, a formula, a physical constant, an instrument accuracy requirement, an evidence grade, or a defined term, for the reason stated at the end of the Schedule: those are not matters of law.

Schedule of Jurisdictional Entries¶

What this Schedule is, in plain terms. This standard is written to be adopted by any jurisdiction. The science in it does not change from one jurisdiction to the next, because the latent heat of vaporization of water does not change at a state line. What changes is the law: which agency holds authority, what minimum water pressure is codified, at how many megawatts the rule attaches, what counts as one service connection.

Rather than write one jurisdiction's statutes into the text and force every other jurisdiction to find and replace them, this standard states a numbered entry at each of those points — forty-two of them, numbered J-1 through J-42. An entry is a labeled blank line with a stated default. The adopting authority writes its own provision on the line. Nothing else in the standard moves.

Each entry carries its label wherever it appears. In the body of the document an entry looks like this:

J-13design pressure 

The black tab is the entry number, for cross-reference to this Schedule. The grey tab says what the entry is. The rule is where the citation or value goes. A parenthetical after it, where one appears, states exactly what to write. No reader has to turn back to this page to learn what an entry means.

Twenty-two of the forty-two carry a working default, taken from published engineering consensus rather than from any one jurisdiction — 35 psi, 20 psi, 1.5 gpm per connection, 0.6 gpm, 200 gallons, 75 megawatts, 36 months, and the rest, each shown in the Default column below. A line left blank takes its default, and the standard still operates. Subsection (n) of the rule text says so. That is what separates this Schedule from a form with holes in it.

No entry reaches the engineering. Not a classification on either axis, not a measurement boundary, not a formula, not a physical constant, not an instrument accuracy class, not an evidence grade, not a defined term. The closing box of this Schedule lists what is fixed and cannot be varied by any entry on it.

The adopting authority completes this Schedule. Each entry receives the citation, name, or value indicated in the description column. An entry for which the jurisdiction has no corresponding provision or body is completed none; an entry the jurisdiction adopts at its stated default is completed default adopted or left blank.

Part I — Purpose and Statutory Frame¶

Procedural posture. This document requests adoption of a rule of general applicability under J-4procedural route  (the provision under which a rule is proposed, petitioned for, or initiated) and the general rulemaking authority of the adopting authority at J-2rulemaking authority  (the provision conferring general rulemaking authority). It opens no new subject. It completes a measurement framework by supplying the definitional sections — and the statutory basis for each — without which the framework cannot be applied uniformly.

1.1 What is already before the adopting authority¶

The authority and the obligation to measure large-load consumption are, in most jurisdictions that have legislated on large electric loads at all, already codified. This standard operates entirely inside the existing statutory framework of the adopting jurisdiction. It does not add new demands. It completes the definitions the framework requires in order to be enforceable, and it directs the adopting authority to the provisions of its own law that already require them.

The distinction matters procedurally. A proposal asking an authority to regulate something it has not been asked to regulate is a new subject. A proposal supplying the operative definitions for a measurement the authority is already directed to obtain is completion of an existing framework. This is the second kind. Every subsection proposed in Part V attaches to an obligation that exists in the statute, rule, or directive entered at J-5interconnection statute, J-7implementing rule, J-8cost-allocation statute, J-10usage-reporting requirement, J-11audit directive, J-12submetering authority, J-13design pressure, J-16operating pressure, J-17capacity per connection, and J-20engineering plan submittal; none creates a new obligation, and none requires an instrument the jurisdiction's law has not already defined.

1.2 The codified authority and obligation¶

1.2.1 The large-load interconnection statute¶

Enter at J-5interconnection statute  (the statute establishing interconnection standards for large electric loads), with its effective date, which directs the adopting authority to establish interconnection standards for any new or expanded large load exceeding J-6applicability threshold  (the threshold, in megawatts; default 75 MW) at a single site, including demonstrated site control, disclosure obligations, and detailed site-related studies.

Two features of such a statute matter directly, and the standard is drafted to operate with either present or absent.

First, where the statute contemplates a water study as a recurring deliverable rather than a one-time representation, it presupposes a measurement method capable of producing a comparable figure period over period. A figure not produced by a stated method against a stated boundary cannot be compared to the figure produced three months earlier, and a quarterly series of such figures records nothing. Second, where the statute routes large-load disclosure through the transmission provider and the grid operator entered at J-9grid operator  (the balancing authority, ISO, or RTO) rather than through the load's own unverified statement, it has already adopted the integrity principle this standard applies to the water leg. The rule proposed here supplies the water-side and electrical-side methods that make both features operable.

1.2.2 The implementing rules¶

Enter at J-7implementing rule  (the administrative rule implementing the statute at J-5, with its adoption date). The section proposed here is drafted to operate alongside that rule and is proposed for placement adjacent to it. Where the implementing rule establishes an interconnection fee, study fee, or financial security obligation, enter those provisions at the same entry; they are the basis against which cost causation is measured under subsection (l).

1.2.3 The survey and its documented failure¶

Enter at J-10usage-reporting requirement  (any existing survey, inventory, or self-reporting requirement applicable to computational facilities, with its compliance rate if known).

Where such a requirement exists and its compliance record is known, that record is the empirical premise of Part II of this document. In the jurisdiction from which this standard was first developed, a legislatively established survey requiring data centers to report water and power usage produced compliance from fewer than ten percent of data centers, and that documented failure was the stated basis of the verification directive that followed. A jurisdiction with no such requirement and therefore no such record is not thereby in a better position; it is in the position of having no record at all, which Part II addresses on the same reasoning.

1.2.4 The audit or verification directive¶

Enter at J-11audit directive  (any executive order, legislative directive, or agency order requiring verification or audit of computational-facility consumption, with its date).

A directive of that kind ordinarily requires three quantities: a water consumption figure, annual and peak; an identified and legally authorized source; and a stated cooling technology. What such a directive does not, and by its nature cannot, specify is how those three quantities are to be measured, by whom, against what facility class, and in what form the result is transmitted to the several authorities that need it. Those are engineering and definitional questions, and they are the subject of Part V. Where no directive exists, the same three quantities remain the quantities any evaluation of a large load requires, and Part V supplies the method by which they are produced.

1.2.5 Water metering authority already exists¶

Enter at J-12submetering authority  (the provision recognizing a point-of-use submeter downstream of a master meter).

Point-of-use submetering — a meter placed downstream of a master meter to measure a defined use — is a recognized instrument and a recognized regulatory concept in essentially every jurisdiction that regulates water service, whether by rule, by tariff, or by agreement with the supplying system. The concept, the instrument, and the regulatory recognition all exist. The proposed rule applies that codified concept to a defined point: the feed to an evaporative circuit. Where the jurisdiction has no codified provision, the entry is completed to state that the submeter is installed by agreement with the supplying system, which is the default and is sufficient.

1.2.6 The system-integrity floors already exist¶

Public water systems are bound by both a design standard and an operating standard. Enter the governing provisions:

J-13design pressure  — Design standard. The distribution system must be designed to maintain a minimum pressure of psi at all points within the distribution network at flow rates of at least J-14design flow per connection gpm per connection. (Defaults: not less than 35 psi; 1.5 gpm per connection.)

J-15fire-flow pressure  — Fire-flow standard. Where the system provides firefighting capability, it must also be designed to maintain at least psi under combined fire and drinking-water flow conditions. (Default: 20 psi.)

J-16operating pressure  — Operating standard. All public water systems must be operated to provide a minimum pressure of psi throughout the distribution system under normal operating conditions, and to maintain at least psi during emergencies such as firefighting. (Defaults: 35 psi normal; 20 psi emergency.)

J-17capacity per connection  — Capacity standard. Minimum source, service-pump, and storage capacity, stated per connection by system category. (Defaults: 0.6 gpm production, 200 gallons total storage, 100 gallons elevated storage or 20 gallons of pressure-tank capacity where the jurisdiction permits one, and 2.0 gpm service pump, each per connection. Where the jurisdiction states capacity by system category, the entry states the category applicable to the supplying system.)

The design floor and the operating floor are distinct obligations, and both are relevant. A distribution system may have been lawfully designed to its design pressure years before a large evaporative load appeared on it, and still fail the operating requirement once that load is added at coincident summer peak. A measurement standard that produces the added consumptive draw at the point and time it occurs is what allows either obligation to be evaluated before the failure rather than after it.

1.3 The four specifications the framework does not yet contain¶

Where an audit is mandatory and the metering concept is codified, what the framework still does not specify is:

  1. The measurement boundary — the physical point at which water and electrical consumption are read. Proposed subsection (f) on the water leg; subsection (i) on the electrical leg.
  2. Who is competent to verify it — the qualification and independence of the party whose reading the jurisdiction relies upon. Proposed subsection (g).
  3. The facility classes it must read — the workload and cooling classifications that determine which measurement method applies and against which peak the facility is assessed. Proposed subsections (d), (e), and (h).
  4. How its output resolves jurisdiction — the single standardized record readable by every authority with jurisdiction over a component of the site's draw, together with the realized-figure and cost-causation consequences that follow. Proposed subsections (j), (k), and (l).

1.4 What this standard does not do¶

For clarity of the record, and to narrow what the adopting authority must decide:

  • It does not propose a new instrument. Every meter specified is an off-the-shelf device already used for revenue billing or tenant allocation.
  • It does not propose inspection of software, source code, model weights, customer algorithms, or tenant data. Measurement operates only on detectable physical electrical, thermal, and hydraulic quantities. Proposed subsection (b)(4) states this as an operative limit of the rule.
  • It does not propose a cap, a moratorium, a siting restriction, or a denial standard. It proposes measurement. What the jurisdiction does with a measured figure is the jurisdiction's determination.
  • It does not name, target, or evaluate any operator. Every figure used is drawn from published vendor-neutral specifications.
  • It does not seek any exclusive role for the author, or for any vendor, utility, or verifier, in performing the measurement it proposes.
  • It does not propose a remedy, a threshold, or a penalty. Those are governmental determinations belonging to the bodies holding the applicable statutory authority. The author does not hold that authority and does not seek it.

Part II — Why Self-Reporting Is Insufficient as a Matter of Record,
and Why Third-Party Verification Is Required¶

2.1 Self-reporting has already failed in this exact context, on the record¶

Where the jurisdiction has an existing self-reporting requirement at J-10usage-reporting requirement, its compliance record is the first evidence in this analysis. In the jurisdiction from which this standard was developed, a survey measuring data-center water and power usage produced compliance from fewer than ten percent of data centers, and that documented failure was the stated basis of the verification directive that followed.

The significance is not that the compliance rate was low. It is that the failure was method failure, not enforcement failure. The survey asked the measured party to produce its own figure. Fewer than one in ten produced anything at all, and for those that did, the State received an assertion whose accuracy it had no independent means to test. A standard that permits the measured party to produce its own unverified figure repeats the precise failure an audit is ordered to cure — and does so at a moment when very large volumes of interconnection requests are pending on the answer.

2.2 No other regulated connection to shared public infrastructure is certified by the connecting party's own word¶

An electrical interconnection is inspected and verified independently. A plumbing tie-in to a public water system is inspected and verified independently. A commercial revenue meter is set, sealed, and read by the utility, not by the customer. Elevators, boilers, fire-suppression systems, and structural welds are all verified by a party other than the one whose work is being verified.

The integrity principle is uniform across utility regulation: the party consuming a shared public resource does not self-certify its own consumption. Where the statute at J-5interconnection statute routes large-load disclosures through the transmission provider and the grid operator at J-9grid operator rather than through the load's unverified self-statement, the statute itself embodies the principle. The water measurement should follow the same principle the electrical measurement already follows.

Stated as a question the adopting authority can answer directly: if the electrical interconnection of a load at the threshold entered at J-6applicability threshold is not accepted on the applicant's own word, there is no principled reason the water consumption of the same load at the same site on the same day should be.

2.3 The requirement stated¶

The standard must require independent third-party measurement or verification. The measurement shall be performed by, or verified by, a qualified party independent of the entity being measured, in accordance with the specifications proposed in Part V. The requirement is not satisfied by a figure produced solely by the entity being measured.

Two elements of that sentence are load-bearing and are defined precisely in Part V. Qualified is defined by reference to the independence-of-judgment standard entered at J-22verifier independence standard  (the jurisdiction's own provision, or the default at ISO/IEC 17020 and 17025) — not by any new licensing scheme, registry, or certification program, none of which presently exists for this field and none of which this standard asks any authority to create. In accordance with the specifications proposed means the verifier reads the boundaries defined in proposed subsections (f) and (i), against the classification defined in subsections (d), (e), and (h), and reports in the form defined in subsection (j). A verification performed against no stated method is not a verification; it is a second assertion.

2.4 The cost of the alternative¶

If the standard permits self-reported figures, three consequences follow mechanically:

  1. The figure is unfalsifiable. No reviewing agency can distinguish a conservative estimate from an optimistic one, because no physical reading exists against which either can be checked.
  2. Cost causation cannot be enforced. The provision entered at J-8cost-allocation statute  (the cost-allocation statute) assigns interconnection and infrastructure cost to the load that causes it. Assignment requires a measured quantity of causation. Without one, the default is socialization onto the general rate base — the outcome such a statute is enacted to prevent.
  3. Failures surface only physically. A municipal system that cannot hold the operating pressure at J-16operating pressure under a new coincident load does not announce the fact administratively. It announces it as a pressure complaint, a brown-water event, or a precautionary notice under J-21public notification, after the fact, to residents who had no part in the transaction.

Part III — The Independence-of-Judgment Standard Already in Force¶

3.1 The standard that governs who may verify a measurement already exists¶

No jurisdiction needs a new definition of a competent verifier. One already exists, in two places, and the operative quality both name is independence of judgment.

In the jurisdiction's own law. Most jurisdictions that permit third-party inspection of regulated work define the inspecting party as one determined to be qualified by reason of facilities, personnel, experience, demonstrated reliability, and independence of judgment to inspect work for compliance with approved plans, documentation, a compliance-control program, and the applicable code. Enter the governing provision at J-22verifier independence standard  (the provision of the jurisdiction's own law stating the independence-of-judgment standard for a third-party verifier; where the jurisdiction has none, the entry takes its default).

In international consensus standards. ISO/IEC 17020, governing bodies performing inspection, and ISO/IEC 17025, governing testing and calibration laboratories, each state impartiality and independence requirements of the same substance. These are the default basis for J-22verifier independence standard, and they apply in any jurisdiction, whether or not that jurisdiction has a provision of its own.

Citation note. Where a jurisdiction's provision has been renumbered by amendment, the adopting authority should read the entry at J-22verifier independence standard as being to the definition, which has been stable in substance, rather than to a paragraph number that has moved.

The structure of that definition is worth stating plainly, because it is the structure the measurement standard borrows: verification of compliance is entrusted to a party independent of the party whose work is being verified, precisely because independence of judgment is the quality that makes a verification reliable. Facilities, personnel, experience, and demonstrated reliability establish that the verifier can measure. Independence of judgment establishes that the measurement can be relied upon.

3.2 A self-report cannot meet that standard¶

A data center's self-report of its own consumption cannot meet the standard, and the reason is definitional rather than reputational. The measured party is not independent of the measurement; it has a direct interest in the result. A figure produced by the party being measured therefore lacks the independence of judgment that the law at J-22verifier independence standard already requires of a verification, and is an assertion rather than a verified measurement. No inference of bad faith is required to reach that conclusion, and none is offered here. The point is structural: the same figure produced by an interested party and by a disinterested party are not the same evidentiary object, and the law has already decided which one a verification is.

3.3 The approval criteria are also already written¶

The law goes further than defining independence; it states operationally what an independent verifier must attest. Enter the governing provision at J-23verifier attestation criteria  (the provision stating what an independent verifier must attest on application or approval). A party seeking approval ordinarily must submit a signed statement that, among other things:

  • its board, as a body, and its managerial and inspection personnel, as individuals, are free to exercise independence of judgment in the performance of their duties;
  • its activities in discharging its responsibilities will not result in financial benefit via stock ownership or other financial interest in any producer, supplier, or vendor of the products involved, other than through standard fees for services rendered;
  • it will consistently and uniformly implement the governing interpretations of the standards and rules; and
  • it will not provide design services for the party whose work it inspects.

Every one of those four attestations transposes directly onto the measurement proposed here, and the fourth is the most important. A party that designed the cooling plant, specified the misting skid, or engineered the electrical distribution is not positioned to verify the consumption of what it designed. The standard proposed in subsection (g) adopts these criteria by reference rather than inventing new ones, which has the additional advantage that a verifier in the jurisdiction already knows what the attestation means.

3.4 This is not a new or special rule for data centers¶

It is the same standard applied to the same kind of question. A directive entered at J-11audit directive ordering an audit orders a verification, and an audit ordered by a government falls within the same scope that makes verification of compliance a matter of independent judgment throughout utility and building regulation. Where the self-reporting method has already failed on the record at J-10usage-reporting requirement, the reason it cannot be relied upon is the reason the law at J-22verifier independence standard already gives — a party cannot supply the independent judgment required to verify its own compliance.

Terminology adopted throughout this standard. This standard uses qualified independent third party, and never certified third party. The distinction is deliberate and material: there is at present no certification, license, or registry specific to computational-load and evaporative-consumption measurement in any jurisdiction, and a rule that required one would be unenforceable on the day it was adopted.

Qualified is a substantive test the adopting authority or the receiving authority applies to a given party against the criteria in subsection (g) — facilities, personnel, experience, demonstrated reliability, independence of judgment, and the absence of a design or financial relationship with the measured entity. It is satisfiable today, by licensed professional engineers, testing and metering firms, and independent inspection agencies already operating in every jurisdiction to which this standard may be submitted.

Measurement without classification is not uniform. The classification stated in this Part is adopted as operative rule text at proposed subsections (d) and (e).

IV.C The Deterministic Heat-and-Water Chain¶

Data-center energy use is not an abstract metric. It is a physical event connected to local grid and water infrastructure through a deterministic three-link chain. Each link is an identity or a bounded engineering ratio, not an assumption, and each is derived with worked arithmetic in Annex I.

IV.C.1 Link one — electrical draw to heat¶

Computational silicon performs no mechanical work and stores no significant energy. Essentially all electrical power delivered to it is converted to heat:

Q [BTU/hr] = P [W] x 3.412142 equivalently Q [BTU/hr] = P [MW] x 3,412,142

This is a unit identity, not a model. A 150 kW rack rejects 511,821 BTU/hr — approximately 42.7 tons of refrigeration at the 12,000 BTU/hr refrigeration ton, or the continuous output of roughly five to six typical residential furnaces rated at 80,000–100,000 BTU/hr input.

IV.C.2 Link two — heat to cooling energy¶

Removing that thermal energy requires cooling power. In high-density operations, cooling and facility overhead account for approximately 30–40 percent of total facility electricity; air cooling demands roughly an additional 0.3–0.5 W of cooling power for every 1 W of active IT load. Expressed as power usage effectiveness:

P_total facility = P_IT x PUE where PUE = 1.15-1.25 (dry, mild) to 1.50-1.70+ (extreme ambient)

The reported electrical figure must therefore be the gross facility figure. Reporting critical IT load alone understates the physical draw on the grid by the entire cooling and infrastructure fraction — which is to say, by exactly the fraction that grows during the summer hours when the grid is most constrained.

IV.C.3 Link three — cooling to water consumption¶

Where heat is rejected evaporatively, water is consumed by phase change. The consumed mass follows from the latent heat of vaporization:

m_water [lb/hr] = Q [BTU/hr] / h_fg V_water [gal/hr] = m_water / 8.34

At h_fg ≈ 1,050 BTU/lb (typical of tower operating temperatures):

1 MW of heat rejected evaporatively = 3,412,142 / 1,050 / 8.34 = 389.6 gal/hr = 9,352 gal/MW-day

Two independent methods agree on this figure. The latent-heat derivation above gives approximately 390 gal/hr per MW. The cooling-tower rule of thumb — evaporation of approximately one percent of recirculating flow per 10 °F of range — gives 409 gal/hr per MW. The consumptive evaporation rate for a fully evaporative facility is therefore approximately 390–410 gal/hr per MW of heat rejected, and this standard uses 390 gal/hr per MW as the working value.

Evaporation is not the whole of the water draw. Total makeup water fed to an evaporative circuit is the sum of evaporation, drift, and blowdown:

IV.D Axis 1 — Workload Profile Classification (Classes W-1, W-2, W-3)¶

Axis 1 classifies a facility, or a circuit within a facility, by the shape of its electrical load over time. Shape is the property that determines how much capacity a utility must hold in reserve, and it is the property that a time-averaged reading destroys.

Class W-1 — Continuous High-Utilization (AI Training / HPC)¶

Class W-1 defines computational workloads characterized by a high, flat, and uninterrupted electrical load shape. It represents the most energy-dense and thermally intense operational class in modern data-center infrastructure.

1. Electrical load shape and utilization. Class W-1 workloads run for long, continuous execution intervals, frequently measured in days or weeks. Unlike variable cloud or web traffic, the load shape does not fluctuate: the peak demand and the mean demand over the interval are virtually identical. Stated as the measured quantity that defines the class, the load factor — mean demand divided by peak demand over the full reporting period, at one-minute integration — is 0.95 or greater. Utilization against a device’s published thermal design power is not the test, because a published device rating is not a measured facility load; the test is computed entirely from readings taken at the transport boundary.

2. Hardware and power density. Class W-1 is typical of AI model-training superclusters — integrated 8-GPU nodes such as H100, H200, or B200 class systems — and of continuous high-performance computing. Rack power densities range from 40 to 80+ kW per rack, extending to 150+ kW per rack in next-generation architectures. An integrated 8-GPU server node alone draws 6.5 to 7.5 kW.

3. Thermal and water footprint. Because computational silicon converts essentially all electrical input into heat, Class W-1 facilities produce continuous heat rejection at their peak rate. Beyond the 25–30 kW per rack threshold, air cooling fails, making direct liquid cooling or cold plates physically mandatory. When paired with evaporative cooling towers, this continuous thermal output drives sustained, maximum municipal water consumption — on the order of 390 gal/hr per MW of rejected heat, continuously, for the duration of the run (Annex I).

4. Measurement and regulatory rule. Because peak and average draw are nearly identical, Class W-1 loads cannot be concealed behind time-averaging — but neither can they be understated by it. Class W-1 circuits must be metered at the secondary distribution bus or PDU level (the M2 transport boundary) using revenue-grade current-transformer submeters compliant with ANSI C12.20 accuracy Class 0.1, 0.2 or 0.5, or IEC 62053-22 accuracy class 0.1S, 0.2S or 0.5S, together with open server telemetry, so that continuous grid and thermal strain is accurately attributed to the circuits producing it.

Why W-1 is the reference class for water. A continuous class has no diversity. There is no hour of the day in which a training cluster's heat rejection falls off, and therefore no hour in which its evaporative makeup demand falls off. Utility planning practice that assumes a load factor materially below unity is inapplicable to Class W-1, and a water study that applies one to a W-1 facility understates the demand by the inverse of the assumed factor.

Class W-2 — Burst / Variable (AI Inference, Cloud, and Colocation)¶

Class W-2 defines computational workloads whose power draw rises and falls dynamically with real-time user query volume and transactional demand. Unlike flat, continuous training runs, Class W-2 represents a highly non-uniform, bursty load profile that presents distinct challenges for grid and municipal water infrastructure.

1. Electrical load shape and utilization dynamics.

  • Demand-driven swings. Measured demand swings rapidly, commonly across a range of roughly 40 percent to 100 percent of the circuit’s own measured peak, following real-time API query traffic, model inference requests, or cloud application spikes. The class is defined by a load factor below 0.95 over the full reporting period, which is the complement of the Class W-1 test and requires no reference to a device rating.
  • Peak-versus-average disparity. The instantaneous peak electrical draw exceeds the long-term mean, and the ratio of the two is the load factor that classifies the circuit. Because queries cluster during business hours and active usage windows, Class W-2 creates steep, high-amplitude power ramps against a lower nighttime baseline.

2. Hardware architecture and rack density. Deployed across high-density GPU inference nodes, specialized ASIC accelerators, and multi-tenant cloud application servers, operating primarily in the 20 to 40 kW per rack bracket. This is the physical transition zone where conventional air cooling reaches its thermodynamic limit (25–30 kW per rack), requiring rear-door liquid heat exchangers or localized cold plates to handle rapid thermal spikes.

3. Thermal rejection and environmental strain.

  • Coincident peak stress. The sudden power spikes of Class W-2 generate localized, high-intensity thermal surges. User query peaks often coincide directly with afternoon grid stress and high ambient summer temperatures, magnifying the cooling infrastructure's power and water requirements at precisely the worst hour.
  • Variable evaporative demand. Cooling water consumption scales linearly with thermal rejection. Evaporative loss from cooling towers therefore fluctuates sharply alongside query volume, creating sudden, unpredictable draws on municipal water supply systems — a demand shape that municipal storage and pumping design does not anticipate from a single connection.

4. The regulatory rule — mandatory peak evaluation.

  • Failure of annual averaging. Reporting Class W-2 facilities as a single annual mean or blended average materially understates the physical strain placed on the system. An annual average smooths away the volatile spikes that actually force utilities to build peak generation, expand transformer capacity, and reserve municipal water pressure.
  • Assessment rule. Any facility or circuit classified as Class W-2 must be evaluated and assessed against its measured peak draw, not its annual mean. Where a filing states an average, it must state the peak alongside it and identify the measurement interval from which the peak was taken.

5. M2 transport-boundary instrumentation.

  • Tier 1 — bus / branch submeter. Revenue-grade digital power meters compliant with ANSI C12.20 or IEC 62053-22 installed at the secondary PDU bus feeding inference pods.
  • Tier 2 — server telemetry. Real-time firmware-level power and thermal polling via standard open hardware protocols (intelligent PDUs, IPMI, and DMTF Redfish).

Class W-3 — Mixed / Multi-Tenant Facility¶

Class W-3 defines computational facilities that house a combination of distinct workload types — AI model training, AI inference, cloud applications, and transactional databases — under a single roof. It represents the composite operational reality of commercial colocation centers, hyperscale multi-tenant campuses, and hybrid enterprise facilities.

1. The composite and heterogeneous load profile. Rather than exhibiting a single uniform or bursty signature across the entire site, Class W-3 facilities contain distinct server halls or PDU panels dedicated to different tenant requirements. Within the same facility, power draw varies by an order of magnitude across pods — from steady enterprise compute racks (5–15 kW per rack) to high-density AI inference pods (20–40 kW per rack) to hyper-dense AI training clusters (40–80+ kW per rack).

2. The regulatory rule — sum of constituent classes.

  • Failure of blended averaging. Reporting a Class W-3 facility through a single aggregate fence-meter reading averages away the specific physical impacts of each tenant hall. It conceals whether grid and cooling-water strain originates from a small subset of high-density GPU circuits or from general cloud hosting.
  • Assessment rule. A Class W-3 facility cannot be measured or assessed as a single blended average. It must be evaluated as the sum of its constituent workload classes (W-1, W-2, and baseline uniform compute) resolved per circuit, per PDU panel, or per tenant pod.

3. Multi-zone thermal and cooling infrastructure. Because different server halls reject heat at vastly different rates, Class W-3 facilities require multi-zone cooling control. As demonstrated in the reference panel schedule at Exhibit 4 (PDU-REF-1, drawing GRUS-GRID-005), a small handful of non-uniform GPU circuits on a mixed PDU panel drive approximately 115 kWth of approximately 142.5 kWth total — 80.7 percent of thermal rejection and therefore of cooling-water demand — and approximately 168 A of approximately 256 A of peak draw, while surrounding storage and database circuits draw flat, minimal fractions. Evaluating the panel by its constituent classes attributes this strain directly to the circuits causing it.

4. Transport-boundary instrumentation (M2 isolation). To disaggregate a Class W-3 facility into its constituent workload classes without interfering with private tenant operations, consumption is measured at the internal transport boundary: Tier 1 revenue-grade submetering at the secondary PDU buses feeding individual tenant halls or compute pods, and Tier 2 open telemetry via intelligent PDUs, IPMI, and DMTF Redfish. Black-box privacy protection applies without exception: measurement operates strictly on detectable physical electrical and thermal characteristics, delivering full cost-attribution visibility without inspecting proprietary source code, customer algorithms, model weights, or private tenant data.

IV.E Axis 2 — Cooling Method Classification (Classes C-1 through C-5)¶

Axis 2 classifies a facility, or a circuit within a facility, by where and whether water is evaporated in the course of rejecting heat. Axis 2 — not the facility's self-description — determines whether a secondary water meter is required.

Class C-1 — Air-Cooled / No Evaporative Circuit¶

Class C-1 defines cooling architectures where computational heat is rejected entirely to the ambient air using dry coolers or air-cooled chillers, without an evaporative water loop in normal operation. It is the dry baseline of Axis 2.

  • Infrastructure and mechanics. Standard mechanical refrigeration or dry-fluid coolers — CRAC/CRAH units, air-cooled chillers, and air-side economizers — transferring thermal energy directly from IT equipment to ambient air.
  • Water consumption profile. A facility operating strictly within Class C-1 consumes near-zero cooling water in day-to-day operation, because no water is evaporated to atmosphere.
  • Conditional exemption. A facility claims Class C-1 status only so long as it operates no evaporative circuit and no evaporative add-on.
  • Reclassification trigger. If an air-cooled facility installs or engages any evaporative-assist mechanism — condenser-coil misting, spray pre-cooling, or adiabatic wetted pads during extreme summer ambient peaks — it automatically reclassifies as Class C-4 (Hybrid / Evaporative-Assisted).
  • Submetering requirement. On entering Class C-4 for that circuit, the facility becomes subject to the mandatory secondary point-of-use water submeter under the Two-Meter Method (proposed subsection (f)).

Density limits and electrical trade-offs. Conventional air cooling reaches its physical boundary at approximately 25–30 kW per rack; facilities housing AI training racks at 40–80+ kW per rack physically cannot operate under pure Class C-1 air cooling and are forced into liquid-cooled regimes. And while Class C-1 preserves municipal water, air-cooled chillers require higher electrical input per watt of IT load at high ambient temperature — typically 0.3 to 0.5 W of cooling power per 1 W of IT load — exchanging water consumption for higher peak electrical demand on the grid. Class C-1 is therefore not the costless option; it is the option that moves the burden from the water utility to the electric utility, and it must be reported as such.

Class C-2 — Closed-Loop / Recirculating Non-Evaporative¶

Class C-2 defines cooling architectures using a sealed recirculating loop to transfer heat away from computational hardware without evaporating water to atmosphere.

1. Core mechanics — the sealed loop. Class C-2 systems use sealed internal fluid loops (treated water, glycol, or dielectric fluid) running through liquid-to-liquid heat exchangers, rear-door heat exchangers, or direct liquid cooling cold plates mounted to CPU/GPU dies. Because the internal loop is sealed, water recirculates continuously with zero operational evaporation or blowdown discharge from that loop.

2. The outdoor rejection condition — closing the C-2 / C-3 loophole. A closed indoor liquid loop is not, on its own, sufficient to claim Class C-2 status. Class C-2 holds only if heat rejection to the outside is also non-evaporative — dry fluid coolers or air-cooled radiators. If a facility uses a closed indoor liquid loop but rejects that heat outdoors using evaporative cooling towers, adiabatic wetted media, or spray ponds, the facility is not Class C-2. It is Class C-3, because the consumptive water loss occurs at the outdoor heat-rejection stage.

3. Extreme ambient heat and the Class C-4 transition. During extreme ambient dry-bulb heat, outdoor dry coolers often cannot reject heat from high-density racks within design approach. If a Class C-2 facility engages any evaporative assist — spray misting, condenser-coil pre-cooling, or wetted adiabatic pads — the facility automatically changes class to Class C-4 for that circuit, and that circuit becomes immediately subject to the secondary point-of-use submeter under the authority entered at J-12submetering authority  (the point-of-use submetering authority).

4. Facility density and grid footprint. Closed-loop internal systems are essential to supporting AI training (40–80+ kW/rack) and next-generation (150+ kW/rack) pods that exceed the air-cooling limit. When paired with dry outdoor heat rejection, true Class C-2 facilities preserve municipal water resources but, like Class C-1, require higher outdoor fan and compressor energy during peak summer heat.

Class C-3 — Evaporative / Cooling Towers / Adiabatic¶

Class C-3 defines architectures where heat is rejected directly to atmosphere by evaporating water. It is the most water-consumptive cooling classification and the primary technical focus of the Two-Meter Method.

1. Core mechanics and water loss. Class C-3 systems rely on open or closed-circuit cooling towers, evaporative condensers, wetted-media adiabatic systems, or spray ponds. Thermal energy is transferred to a water loop and rejected as water changes phase from liquid to vapor. Water loss is an unavoidable thermodynamic consequence of operation and consists of:

  • Evaporative loss — water converted to vapor and lost to atmosphere (~390 gal/hr per MW rejected);
  • Drift — entrained droplets carried out of the tower, bounded at roughly 0.005 percent of recirculating flow by drift eliminators, and consumptive;
  • Blowdown / bleed-off — concentrated water discharged to sewer to prevent scale, equal to evaporation divided by (COC − 1), and withdrawn but not consumed.

2. The outdoor heat-rejection rule (distinguishing C-2 from C-3). A common misconception is that a facility with closed indoor liquid loops — direct-to-chip cold plates or rear-door heat exchangers — is automatically a closed-loop facility. The standard establishes the opposite: classification is governed by where the evaporative water loss physically occurs. A closed indoor loop rejecting heat outdoors through evaporative towers or wetted media is Class C-3.

3. The mandatory Two-Meter Method.

  • Primary service-entrance meter — the standard utility master meter measuring total water intake delivered to the property line.
  • Secondary point-of-use submeter — an ordinary, off-the-shelf water meter installed at the feed line entering the evaporative cooling tower or loop.
  • Calculating consumptive loss — metering the evaporative feed directly identifies the water entering the evaporative circuit; the portion of that feed that does not return as metered or conductivity-derived blowdown is the quantity consumed to atmosphere. This eliminates reliance on fictional drain-line estimates.

4. Statutory grounding and municipal system protection. The secondary meter requires no specialized or proprietary hardware; it is the point-of-use submeter recognized at J-12submetering authority . Large continuous evaporative draws can depress local water system pressures; metering Class C-3 feeds ensures the draw can be evaluated against the design and operating pressure floors entered at J-13design pressure, J-14design flow per connection, J-15fire-flow pressure, and J-16operating pressure and the capacity requirements entered at J-17capacity per connection.

Class C-4 — Hybrid / Evaporative-Assisted¶

Class C-4 defines air-cooled or closed-loop systems fitted with supplemental evaporative assist — condenser-coil misting, spray pre-cooling, or wetted adiabatic pads — that engage during high ambient temperature conditions. It is the mechanism that captures evaporative water consumption occurring during extreme summer heat waves, which is exactly when it is least visible and most damaging.

1. Core mechanics and system architecture. Class C-4 systems operate dry under mild ambient conditions but use supplemental water spraying, misting, or adiabatic wetted media to lower inlet air temperature when outdoor heat rises. Typical configurations include direct misting or spray nozzles at outdoor condenser coils; adiabatic wetted pads upstream of dry fluid coolers or air handlers; and secondary evaporative trim chillers or pre-cooling loops.

2. The ambient heat trigger (dry-bulb / wet-bulb dynamics). Summer ambient dry-bulb temperatures in hot climates frequently exceed the heat-rejection capacity of pure air-cooled radiators or dry coolers. By evaporating water into the incoming air stream, Class C-4 systems drive entering air temperature down toward the ambient wet-bulb temperature, allowing high-density pods to reject heat without thermal throttling or shutdown. The achievable depression is bounded by saturation effectiveness:

T_air-off = T_db - e x (T_db - T_wb) e = saturation effectiveness, typically 0.70-0.90

The wet-bulb temperature is a hard floor. No quantity of water sprayed can cool entering air below it, which is why Annex B computes wet-bulb for every ambient case rather than assuming a fixed temperature drop.

3. Closing the dry-facility reporting loophole. Class C-4 closes the loophole in which an operator represents itself on paper as a dry Class C-1 or closed-loop Class C-2 facility to avoid water reporting, while consuming municipal water through add-on sprays during extreme weather. The presence or operation of any evaporative-assist circuit automatically places that circuit into Class C-4. An operator cannot claim C-1 or C-2 status while evaporating water through an add-on.

4. Mandatory point-of-use submetering. Reclassification to Class C-4 immediately triggers the secondary point-of-use submeter: an ordinary commercial water meter at the feed line supplying the misting, spray, or adiabatic pre-cooling circuit, authorized at J-12submetering authority . Water entering the evaporative-assist feed and lost to atmosphere is measured directly at the point of use, eliminating reliance on unverified self-reporting or drain-line estimates.

5. Protecting municipal infrastructure integrity. The evaporative water draw of Class C-4 systems spikes precisely when ambient heat is highest — coinciding with peak municipal water demand and peak grid stress. Independent submetering of Class C-4 circuits ensures that intense summer misting draws can be evaluated against the pressure floors at J-13design pressure and J-16operating pressure and the public storage and pumping capacities at J-17capacity per connection.

Class C-5 — On-Site Power Generation Evaporative Cooling¶

Class C-5 defines facilities operating on-site power generation whose cooling or power-production processes consume water by evaporation. It brings behind-the-meter generation cooling water into the same measurement standard as facility IT cooling.

1. System architecture and generation assets. Applies to data centers operating islanded, prime, or supplemental on-site generation — combustion gas turbines, combined-cycle units, reciprocating internal combustion engines, steam turbines, or microgrids — and captures any generation process that evaporates water to atmosphere, including: combustion turbine inlet-air evaporative fogging or cooling used to recover turbine capacity during hot peaks; steam turbine condenser cooling towers in combined-cycle or CHP configurations; and engine jacket-water and lube-oil evaporative cooling towers.

2. Closing the behind-the-meter water loophole. To bypass grid interconnection queues, large data centers increasingly deploy on-site generation. Because those assets sit behind the meter relative to the electric utility, operators sometimes argue that water used to cool the power plant should be excluded from data-center water audits. Water evaporated to produce electricity on-site is physically identical in municipal impact to water evaporated to cool server racks. It is a consumptive draw on the same local supply, from the same mains, at the same hour, and it must be measured, not omitted because it sits behind an electrical service meter.

3. Statutory alignment with large-load water studies. Where the statute at J-5interconnection statute and the rule at J-7implementing rule require large-load interconnection applicants at or above the threshold at J-6applicability threshold to disclose on-site generation resources and submit detailed site-related water studies, incorporating Class C-5 ensures that any audit under J-11audit directive captures the total true water footprint of the site — both the generation leg and the IT cooling leg.

4. Mandatory secondary submetering. Any water feed routed into an evaporative circuit for on-site power generation must be fed through, and measured by, a secondary point-of-use submeter under proposed subsection (f), using the same codified instrument authority, so that the exact volume evaporated to atmosphere is isolated and can be evaluated against municipal capacity and the pressure floors at J-13design pressure and J-16operating pressure.

IV.G Workload Separation: Uniformity as the Decisive Property¶

The justification for moving the regulatory measurement boundary from the facility fence (M1) to the internal transport boundary (M2) is physical load uniformity.

Uniform loads (steady state). General compute, database, storage, and transactional applications run at low, predictable, steady draws. Their flat thermal and electrical footprints integrate easily into standard utility forecasting, and an averaged reading describes them accurately.

Non-uniform loads (volatile peaks). AI model training (95 percent or greater sustained utilization) and large-model inference (demand-driven fluctuation between 40 and 100 percent of TDP) create highly variable or continuously maximal draws whose peak is the planning-relevant quantity. For these, an averaged reading is not merely imprecise; it describes a facility that does not exist.

The socialization gap. Standard fence meters average volatile, high-amplitude spikes over billing intervals. This hides the physical peaks that force the utility to build grid capacity, upgrade transmission transformers, and secure additional water capacity. The capacity is built and paid for regardless; the only question the averaging resolves is who pays. Where the causing load is invisible, the cost defaults to the general rate base.

The resolution — M2 isolation. Transport-boundary meters resolve power draw per circuit at the internal power distribution unit level. As demonstrated by drawing GRUS-GRID-005 (Exhibit 4), on a single reference PDU panel, four non-uniform AI circuits of eight total circuits drive approximately 168 A of approximately 256 A of peak draw and approximately 115 kWth of approximately 142.5 kWth of heat — and therefore the corresponding majority of cooling-water consumption — while general compute, database, and storage circuits draw a small, steady, uniform fraction. The aggregate fence meter sees only the summed current and cannot resolve that four circuits are responsible. The M2 meter resolves it per circuit.

Drawing GRUS-GRID-001 (Exhibit 1) establishes why this is not an arbitrary point of insertion: the same distribution hierarchy, the same step-down stages, and the same measurement-node structure recur at every scale from generation to the processor die. M2 is not a new kind of node invented for this rule; it is the same kind of node as M1, placed one level in.

What resolution does and does not conclude. The panel schedule resolves where physical strain concentrates. It does not conclude that any facility should be denied service, curtailed, or charged a particular rate. Those are determinations for the adopting authority and the affected utilities on a measured record. This standard supplies the measurement; the jurisdiction draws the conclusion.

(c) Definitions. The following words and terms, when used in this section, have the following meanings unless the context clearly indicates otherwise.

(1) Approach — the difference between the temperature of fluid leaving a heat-rejection device and the governing ambient temperature, which is the ambient dry-bulb temperature for a non-evaporative device and the ambient wet-bulb temperature for an evaporative device.

On the test at paragraph (2). Load factor is used because it is computed entirely from quantities subsection (i) already requires to be measured and retained, and because it measures the property the classification exists to capture. The shape of a load over time, not its magnitude, is what determines how much capacity a utility must hold in reserve and whether a facility’s heat rejection falls off at any hour of the day. A thermal design power rating is not used as the denominator, for the reason subsection (c)(17) states: a published device rating is an input to a Designed or Modeled figure and is never, standing alone, a measured facility load. A ratio whose denominator is a manufacturer’s rating is not a measurement, and a classification resting on one cannot be verified at the boundary.

The threshold of 0.95 is stated numerically, and the two classes are complementary and exhaustive, so that two reviewers applying the test to the same interval data reach the same class. A continuous load at half of its available capacity has a load factor near unity and is Class W-1, which is the correct result: it has no diurnal diversity, and its evaporative demand does not fall at night. Its magnitude is carried separately by the reported peak, which paragraph (2)(C) requires in every case.

The operative content is paragraph (6). A peak that is averaged away is not thereby removed from the system; it is removed only from the record, and the capacity to serve it is built and paid for by someone regardless. The requirement in paragraph (7) to state the integration interval exists because a peak is meaningless without one: a one-minute peak and a fifteen-minute peak describe different physical events, and only the shorter interval resolves a Class W-2 ramp.

A single intake figure at the property line does not measure consumption, because a substantial portion of drawn water is returned to the sanitary sewer and only the evaporated fraction is consumed. The three reported quantities at paragraph (3) are not redundant. Total intake is what the supplying utility must deliver, and is the figure relevant to source, treatment, and pumping capacity. Evaporative feed is what must be delivered at the moment of ambient peak, and is the figure relevant to distribution pressure. Consumptive loss is what never comes back, and is the figure relevant to basin, aquifer, and regional planning. Blowdown is the figure relevant to the wastewater plant, because it returns concentrated in dissolved solids. One number cannot serve four authorities; three metered quantities can serve all four.

Answering the principal engineering objection. The principal objection to water measurement at computational facilities is that consumptive loss cannot be metered because evaporation leaves no stream to meter. The objection is correct about the vapor and irrelevant to the method. Evaporation is measured by difference at the feed, in the same way any consumptive process is measured: by metering what goes in and accounting for what comes back out. Cycles of concentration are determined by conductivity, an ordinary and continuously logged tower measurement, which is why the return fraction is a determinable quantity rather than an assumption. Paragraphs (2) and (6) state one method, not two: (2) gives the arithmetic and (6) states the physical point of measurement and forecloses the drain-line objection.

Relation to the system-integrity floors. The measurement required by this subsection is the quantity necessary to evaluate compliance with the design standard at J-13design pressure and J-14design flow per connection (minimum pressure at all points at the stated flow per connection), the fire-flow standard at J-15fire-flow pressure, the operating standard at J-16operating pressure, and the capacity standard at J-17capacity per connection. Absent a secondary evaporative meter, compliance with those floors can only be asserted, and failure surfaces only when a smaller system is physically overtaxed. The design floor and the operating floor are separate obligations and fail in different ways: a system lawfully designed to its design pressure at the stated flow per connection may still fall below the operating floor once a continuous multi-hundred-gallon-per-hour evaporative draw is added at a single node during a coincident summer peak, because the design case did not contemplate that node. This subsection produces the input to the hydraulic evaluation of that case. It does not itself determine compliance; it makes determination possible.

Part VI — Why These Belong Inside the Measurement¶

6.1 Each subsection specifies the measurement itself¶

Each proposed subsection specifies a necessary element of the measurement: what it measures on the water leg (subsection (f), the secondary evaporative meter and the Two-Meter Method) and on the electrical leg (subsection (i), the two-tier bus submeter and existing telemetry); who verifies it (subsection (g), a qualified party independent of the one being measured); the class it reads (subsections (d), (e), and (h), the two-axis profile); what its reading resolves (subsection (j), the jurisdiction); the evidentiary grade and scope of what is reported (subsection (k)); and the allocation the reading supports (subsection (l)).

Remove any one and the standard cannot be applied uniformly. Without a boundary, two verifiers measure different things. Without a verifier standard, the boundary is read by an interested party. Without classification, the same reading means different things at different facilities. Without a common record, four authorities receive four partial answers. Without evidence grades, a projection and a measurement appear identical on the page. Without allocation, a correct measurement produces no consequence. These are the definitions the framework already requires in order to function, each grounded in a standard or statute already in force in the adopting jurisdiction or in international consensus.

6.2 The burden is minimal and the method is ordinary¶

On the water leg: one commonplace point-of-use water meter at a defined point, added to a facility that already has a primary meter and is, where the statute at J-5interconnection statute applies, already required to submit water studies and to bear interconnection costs. On the electrical leg: revenue-grade submeters of the type already installed for tenant allocation, plus a software configuration exposing telemetry the servers already produce. No novel instrument, no proprietary device, no tear-out, and no inspection of anything a facility has a legitimate interest in protecting.

What it produces is the one quantity presently missing and unverifiable: how much water and how much peak power a large load actually consumes, measured at the point it occurs, verified by a party competent and independent enough to measure it, assessed against the correct facility class.

6.3 What the whole converts¶

Taken together, these subsections convert a mandated audit from a self-report the facility fills out — the method that has already failed, in the jurisdiction from which this standard was developed, at better than ninety percent — into a measurement a government can stand behind: measured at a defined boundary by an ordinary secondary meter and standard bus submeters, verified by a qualified independent party, assessed against a stated facility class, and recorded in a form every affected authority can read.

That is the entire proposal. It does not decide whether any facility should be built, connected, curtailed, or charged. It decides only that the quantities on which those decisions rest will be measured rather than asserted.

6.4 Why one standard, and why the same one¶

A computational facility in one jurisdiction rejects heat by the same physics as a computational facility in another. The latent heat of vaporization of water does not change at a state line. The air-cooling limit at 25 to 30 kW per rack does not change. The wet-bulb floor does not change. A 150 kW rack rejects 511,821 BTU/hr everywhere.

What changes is the law: which body holds jurisdiction, what pressure floor is codified, what threshold attaches, what a connection is defined to be. Those are the forty-two entries on the Schedule, and they are the whole of what changes.

The consequence is that a figure produced under this standard in one jurisdiction is comparable to a figure produced under it in another, which is the property a measurement standard exists to supply and the property no amount of separate local drafting can produce. Where each jurisdiction writes its own measurement method, the figures are not comparable even when they are both honest, and a developer can present a figure produced by the most permissive method available to the authority least equipped to check it. A single method with declared local parameters removes that option without removing any jurisdiction's authority over anything it already governs.

B.2 Thermodynamic Heat and Water Performance Analysis¶

B.2.1 The 25–30 kW per Rack Limit and the Air-Cooling Threshold¶

The airflow envelope. Air cooling (Class C-1) is bounded by the volumetric airflow required to carry the heat away at the available temperature rise, computed as CFM = Q / (K × ΔT) with K = 0.240 × 60 × ρ at the station air density. Measured against the 2,000 to 6,000 CFM a cabinet and tile arrangement delivers in practice, the band in which air cooling becomes impractical is approximately 25 to 30 kW per rack at ordinary temperature rises. Above that band, liquid cooling — cold plates or direct-to-chip, Class C-2 — is required as a consequence of the airflow arithmetic rather than as a design preference. Part IV.B states the envelope with its elevation dependence, and subsection (h)(5) states the test a filing must satisfy.

The 108 °F OEM limit. While equipment may carry an absolute maximum rating of 125–135 °F, major dry-cooler manufacturers specify approximately 108 °F dry-bulb as the maximum recommended continuous operating temperature. Above it, fan power rises sharply and heat-exchanger approach temperatures break down. The matrix above shows why this matters: at 108 °F and 30 percent relative humidity, 28.0 °F of evaporative potential remains available, and a facility can hold full load by engaging an assist — which is precisely the moment it becomes a Class C-4 facility and incurs the obligation of subsection (f).

B.2.2 Evaporative Water Consumption Dynamics¶

Latent heat conversion. Rejecting 1 MW of heat evaporatively requires 3,412,142 BTU/hr of latent capacity. At h_fg ≈ 1,050 BTU/lb this consumes approximately 390 gal/hr per MW — approximately 9,350 gallons per MW-day of pure evaporation, before blowdown. Including blowdown at typical cycles of concentration, total makeup runs approximately 10,700–14,000 gallons per MW-day (Part IV.C.3).

A single 150 kW AI rack. Rejects 511,821 BTU/hr — approximately 42.7 tons of refrigeration, or the continuous output of roughly five to six residential furnaces rated 80,000 to 100,000 BTU/hr input. Under full evaporative rejection it evaporates 1,403 gallons per day, with drift of 7 gallons per day at a 10 °F range, for a consumptive loss of 1,410 gallons per day. Makeup is 1,600 to 2,100 gallons per day across the band of 8 cycles to 3 cycles. Stated against residential demand at 205 gallons per household per day, that is 7.8 to 10.3 households; at 250 gallons it is 6.4 to 8.4, and at 300 gallons it is 5.3 to 7.0. The divisor is stated because the comparison is not reproducible without it.

B.2.3 The Multi-Day Sustained Heat-Wave Risk¶

Coincident infrastructure stress. Continuous 108 °F-plus heat waves force Class C-3 and Class C-4 systems to operate at or near full evaporative capacity for 72 or more consecutive hours. Unlike a single-day peak, a multi-day event gives municipal storage no overnight recovery window, because the facility's draw does not fall at night and residential irrigation demand resumes each morning against depleted elevated storage.

Municipal main depletion. These high-volume, uninterrupted draws depress municipal distribution mains, risking failure of the design and operating pressure floors at J-13design pressure and J-16operating pressure, and, if pressure falls below the emergency floor at J-15fire-flow pressure, triggering the back-siphonage and public-notification consequences that follow under J-21public notification.

B.2.4 Automatic Reclassification in Operation¶

The moment a facility claiming Class C-1 or Class C-2 status engages condenser misting, fogging, or spray pre-cooling to survive high ambient dry-bulb heat, it reclassifies to Class C-4 under subsection (h)(2), and that circuit becomes subject to the secondary point-of-use submeter under the authority at J-12submetering authority. The matrix above identifies the ambient conditions at which this transition predictably occurs, which is why the transition can be audited rather than merely asserted.

C.6 Core Forensic Rules for Heat-Transfer Boundary Audits¶

  1. Governance by Q = UA ΔTlm. Increasing heat-exchanger surface area (A) reduces the approach temperature, but cannot alter or lower the ambient dry-bulb or wet-bulb floor. No quantity of equipment moves the ambient condition.
  2. Rejection of fictional continuous limits. An absolute equipment limit of 125 °F or 135 °F cannot be treated as a recommended continuous operating temperature when the manufacturer specifies 108 °F. A filing that operates the design case at the absolute rating is deficient under subsection (k)(1).
  3. Misting reduction verification. Misting or adiabatic pre-cooling cannot be claimed to produce a fixed 20 °F or 23 °F temperature drop without documented dry-bulb, wet-bulb, and relative-humidity test logs. The claimed drop shall be stated as a saturation effectiveness against the measured wet-bulb, and effectiveness above 0.90 shall be supported by manufacturer test data at the stated ambient condition.
  4. The wet-bulb floor is absolute. Evaporative cooling of any kind — tower, adiabatic pad, or fogging skid — cannot produce an air or water temperature below the ambient wet-bulb. Any filing whose design case implies otherwise is deficient on its face.
  5. Water follows heat, not nameplate. Consumptive water is a function of heat actually rejected evaporatively, not of installed capacity. A facility operating at 60 percent load evaporates approximately 60 percent of the design rate; a facility at continuous 95 percent utilization (Class W-1) does not.

D.1 Key Operational Findings¶

  1. The Class C-4 transition trigger. In the hot/dry case, dry coolers reach their sensible rejection threshold. Engaging spray misting or adiabatic media depresses entering air toward the wet-bulb, maintaining 100 percent IT load capacity — and automatically reclassifying the circuit to Class C-4, triggering mandatory point-of-use submetering under J-12submetering authority. The facility keeps its load by spending municipal water, and the standard's only requirement is that the spending be measured.
  2. Wet-bulb compression in humid conditions. In the hot/humid case, high relative humidity compresses the evaporative potential to as little as 2.5–9 °F. Evaporative efficiency collapses precisely when heat rejection is hardest, forcing centrifugal chillers to carry the load and driving cooling overhead to +0.55 W per 1 W of IT. Water use does not fall in this case; blowdown rises as cycles concentrate, and the electrical draw rises alongside it.
  3. Multi-day extreme strain. During sustained 108 °F-plus conditions, high-density AI clusters consume up to roughly 2,100 gallons per day per 150 kW rack of makeup water while simultaneously imposing their maximum electrical demand. The two peaks are coincident by construction, not by coincidence — both are driven by the same ambient condition on the same afternoon as peak residential demand.
  4. There is no water-free option at extreme ambient. A facility that avoids evaporative water entirely pays for it in electrical overhead at PUE 1.5–1.7 during precisely the hours the grid is most constrained. The two axes trade against each other, and a measurement standard that reads only one of them can be satisfied by moving the burden to the other.

D_combined = D_existing + D_committed + D_project + D_peak-weather
  • D_existing — current baseline system demand (MDD or PHD as applicable to the test).
  • D_committed — approved, permitted, or contracted commitments not yet online.

The fourth term is the one most often omitted, and it is the term that decides the case. A facility's draw evaluated against average municipal demand will nearly always appear absorbable; the same draw evaluated against the municipal system's own worst hour frequently is not.

E.3.3 The six-point proof protocol for a minimum-pressure compliance claim¶

The protocol forbids treating the minimum pressure at J-13design pressure as a generalized conclusion. To demonstrate compliance, a filing must document six elements:

  1. The measurement location — specific address, pipe junction, or hydraulic model node identifier.
  2. The applicable system category — community public water system, wholesale provider, or industrial master-metered system.
  3. The demand condition evaluated — peak hour, maximum day plus data-center peak, coincident fire flow, or other stated case.
  4. The legal text — the specific provision governing the evaluation (J-13design pressure for design, J-16operating pressure for operation, J-17capacity per connection for capacity).
  5. The engineering method — the reproducible methodology used, for example extended-period hydraulic network modeling in EPANET or an equivalent model, field pressure-logger data, or Hazen-Williams friction-loss calculation, with inputs stated.
  6. The evidence grade — the grade under subsection (k)(1) at which each input to the evaluation is supported, and the tolerance declared for each.

E.4.2 The six-tier relationship classification¶

The relationship between a facility's water draw and a system event must be classified under one of six defined categories, and never by timing alone:

  1. No demonstrated correlation — physical, hydraulic, or temporal evidence disproves any connection.
  2. Temporal correlation only — peak draw coincided in time, but modeling or distance demonstrates no hydraulic mechanism.
  3. Engineering correlation — hydraulic modeling shows that intake velocity created transients, scouring velocities, or head loss capable of contributing.
  4. Probable contribution — calculations and physical evidence indicate high-volume pumping combined with existing pipe degradation to induce failure.
  5. Confirmed cause — telemetry, transducer logs, and pipe failure analysis confirm that facility operation caused the pressure drop or failure.
  6. Unresolved — the record lacks sufficient telemetry, meter data, or inspection to support a determination.

E.4.3 Hydraulic mechanisms¶

Scouring velocities and hydraulic transients. Rapid, un-ramped valve openings for cooling-tower makeup or high-pressure adiabatic spray (Classes C-3 and C-4) cause sudden velocity spikes in distribution mains. High velocity scours iron and manganese tuberculation from older pipe walls, discoloring water throughout the pressure zone.

Pressure floor breaches. Sustained evaporative makeup draws during peak summer can pull distribution pressure below the operating floor at J-16operating pressure. Below the emergency floor at J-15fire-flow pressure, back-siphonage risk rises and the public-notification obligations at J-21public notification attach.

Why this protocol protects both directions. A rigorous incident protocol is as valuable to a well-run facility as to a municipality. Without submeter logs and a stated classification method, a facility located near an aging main will be blamed for every discolored-water call in its pressure zone, and will have no evidence with which to answer. With them, the six-tier classification distinguishes ordinary pipe aging from facility-induced strain on the record. Measurement is the only thing that makes exoneration possible.

F.5 Statutory Takeaways¶

  1. Water submetering is already codified. The Two-Meter Method requires no new statutory authority. Point-of-use submeters are a recognized instrument everywhere water service is regulated, by rule, by tariff, or by agreement with the supplying system. Applying that instrument to the evaporative feed produces the physical data needed to evaluate the pressure and capacity floors at J-13design pressure through J-17capacity per connection.
  2. Independent verification is already defined. Rather than creating new administrative definitions, the rule adopts the independence-of-judgment standard at J-22verifier independence standard and the attestation criteria at J-23verifier attestation criteria, with ISO/IEC 17020 and 17025 as the default where the jurisdiction has no provision of its own. That supplies a legal mechanism to execute any audit directive at J-11audit directive and to cure the documented failure of self-reporting at J-10usage-reporting requirement.
  3. One statute drives both boundaries. The provision at J-8cost-allocation statute requires large loads to contribute to interconnection and infrastructure cost recovery. Measuring electrical draw at the M2 transport boundary with revenue-grade instruments supplies the resolution necessary to assign caused cost to the causing load, and to insulate residential and small-business ratepayers from it.
  4. Nothing in this annex transfers jurisdiction. Each authority entered on the Schedule applies its own law to the record it receives under subsection (j). The reconciliation identifies where a determination is required and by whom. It does not make the determination, and no entry on the Schedule authorizes the adopting authority to make another agency's.

Why non-transferability is a rule and not a caution. Two adjacent municipalities can hold entirely different water rights, contract volumes, storage ratios, and peaking factors while appearing identical on a map. I.2 Psychrometric Formulation (Basis of Annex B)¶

All Annex B values are computed at standard atmospheric pressure P = 14.696 psia using the ASHRAE formulation.

Saturation pressure over liquid water, T in degR = degF + 459.67: ln(p_ws) = C8/T + C9 + C10*T + C11*T^2 + C12*T^3 + C13*ln(T) C8 = -1.0440397E4 C11 = 1.289036E-5 C9 = -1.129465E1 C12 = -2.4780681E-9 C10 = -2.7022355E-2 C13 = 6.5459673 Partial pressure: p_w = RH x p_ws(t) Humidity ratio: W = 0.621945 x p_w / (P - p_w) [lb water / lb dry air] Grains per pound: gr/lb = W x 7,000 Enthalpy: h = 0.240*t + W x (1061 + 0.444*t) [BTU/lb dry air] Dew point (32-200 degF), with a = ln(p_w): t_d = 100.45 + 33.193a + 2.319a^2 + 0.17074a^3 + 1.2063 x p_w^0.1984 Thermodynamic wet-bulb t*, solved iteratively: W = [ (1093 - 0.556*t*) x W_s(t*) - 0.240*(t - t*) ] / [ 1093 + 0.444*t - t* ] Station pressure at elevation ft: P_atm = 14.696 x (1 - 6.8754E-6 x ft)^5.2559 Evaporative potential: dT = t_db - t_wb Evaporative air-off: t_out = t_db - e x (t_db - t_wb) e = saturation effectiveness

Worked example (108 °F dry-bulb, 30 percent relative humidity). p_ws(108 °F) yields p_w = 0.30 p_ws; W = 0.01567 lb/lb = 109.7 gr/lb; h = 43.31 BTU/lb; dew point 69.9 °F; thermodynamic wet-bulb 80.0 °F; evaporative potential ΔT = 28.0 °F; air-off at ε = 0.85 is 84.2 °F. A claimed air-off of 83.2 °F at this condition implies ε = 0.89, which is at the upper bound of achievable performance and requires test data under Annex C.6.

108.0 - 0.85 x 28.0 = 84.2 degF (108.0 - 83.2) / 28.0 = 0.886

The governing condition is wet-bulb, not dry-bulb. Two sites of equal dry-bulb temperature are not equally capable of evaporative heat rejection, and a cooler site is frequently the more constrained one. At 95 °F and 20 percent relative humidity at sea level the thermodynamic wet-bulb is 66.0 °F and the evaporative potential is 29.0 °F. At 87 °F and 95 percent relative humidity at sea level the thermodynamic wet-bulb is 85.7 °F and the evaporative potential is 1.3 °F. The second site is eight degrees cooler by dry-bulb and has lost essentially all of its evaporative capacity.

The indiscriminate reading — a few workload classes drive most of the strain. Across the full landscape, the meter reads every class the same way. The result is not uniform: circuits 1–17, AI training plus large-model inference, carry 108.00 kWth of the 135.40 kWth computational load — 79.8 percent — and therefore that share of the cooling-water demand, while general compute, database and storage on circuits 23–33 together are 12.10 kWth, or 8.9 percent. Against M2 gross of 147.40 kWth, which includes the cooling distribution unit, the same three circuits are 73.3 percent. Both denominators are stated because a share without its denominator cannot be checked.

On the current side, those three circuits contribute 150.3 A of the 227.0 A carried on the most heavily loaded phase, or 66.2 percent. On one panel, four circuits produce most of the physical strain on grid and water.

The aggregate fence meter sees the per-phase sum and cannot resolve that four circuits are responsible. The transport-boundary meter (M2) resolves it per circuit. That resolution — indiscriminate across all classes, revealing where strain concentrates — is the entire purpose of the meter. The author draws no conclusion; the jurisdiction does.
